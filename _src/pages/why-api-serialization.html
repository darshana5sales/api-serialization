The case for traceability

Why API serialization matters — beyond the fact that it is now the law

What API serialization is

Serialization is the practice of giving a product a unique identity — a set of standard details that make one pack distinguishable from every other pack, and traceable through the supply chain.

API serialization applies that idea to Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients — bulk drugs, the raw material from which tablets, capsules, creams and injectables are made. Each API pack is assigned a unique identity, encoded in a QR code on its label, so that at any point afterwards you can establish what the pack contains, who made it, when, and where it has been.

A serialization code can be as detailed as a customer or a government requires. In practice it includes the manufacturer's Global Trade Item Number (GTIN), the drug description, the stock-keeping unit, the batch or lot number, the expiry date, the date of production, and the line and plant it was manufactured on.

API, bulk drug, raw material — same thing An Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient is the component of a formulation that produces the intended effect. Everything else in the tablet is excipient. In Indian industry usage it is more often called bulk drug.

What a serialization code carries

Under India's G.S.R. 20(E) guideline the QR code on an API pack must carry eleven specific particulars — from the unique product identification code through to any special storage conditions. Those are set out in full, with what each one means in practice, on the regulation page.

The important structural point is the Serial Shipping Container Code (SSCC). It is issued to GS1 international standards and is unique to each individual container, not to the batch. A fifty-drum batch produces fifty distinct SSCCs. That is what makes traceability granular enough to be useful — you can isolate one drum, not just one batch.

Four reasons it matters

1. Counterfeiting is a supply chain problem, not a packaging problem

Drug counterfeiting has become a global issue, and India's scale and growth make it a target. Where a supply chain has no visibility, it has gaps — and gaps are an open invitation for counterfeiters to introduce fake or low-quality material.

The damage does not stay with the counterfeiter. It attaches to the manufacturer whose name is printed on the drum: their business, their reputation, and their relationship with every downstream customer. And ultimately fake drugs harm patients and erode public trust in the healthcare system as a whole.

2. It is how you prove quality and safety

Serialization guarantees the identity of what you shipped. By implementing a standard set of processes, a manufacturer can demonstrate complete supply chain visibility for their material — not assert it, demonstrate it, with a record behind every pack.

3. Problems get found and contained faster

When something does go wrong, a serialized supply chain lets you identify the source quickly and take preventive action before the damage widens. Without it, you are reconstructing history from paperwork while the affected material keeps moving.

4. End-to-end traceability helps everyone downstream

Traceability is not only a manufacturer's benefit. Every member of the chain gains from it — better inventory management, faster verification of what a drum actually contains, quicker recalls, and fewer supply delays caused by uncertainty about material that cannot be identified with confidence.

The cost of staying unserialized

The regulatory penalty is the obvious risk. It is rarely the expensive one.

  • Export orders. International buyers increasingly treat scannable traceability as a baseline qualification. A pack that cannot be scanned raises a question at exactly the moment you want none.
  • Audit findings. Inspectors now look for the QR code by default. An observation on an audit report costs far more time to close out than it would have cost to prevent.
  • Customer confidence. Formulation manufacturers are under their own traceability obligations. A supplier who cannot support them creates friction that eventually gets solved by changing supplier.
  • Recall exposure. Without container-level identity, a problem in one drum becomes a problem across the whole consignment, because you cannot prove which drums were unaffected.
At present there is no standard set of rules across the API industry for track and trace, and it remains largely unorganised. That means no visibility in the supply chain, and a large information gap — which is precisely what invites counterfeiters into the market. From the industry brief behind this platform

Who this applies to

If you manufacture or import an Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient in India, the QR code requirement applies to you at every packaging level. In practice that means:

  • API and bulk drug manufacturers selling into the domestic market
  • Importers bringing API into India, who carry the same obligation
  • Pharmaceutical exporters who additionally need GTIN and NDC-standard data for international shipments
  • Contract manufacturing organisations running multiple product lines for multiple clients
  • Quality and regulatory teams who have to produce the evidence when an auditor asks
