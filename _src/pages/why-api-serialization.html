The case for traceability

Why API serialization matters — beyond the fact that it is now the law

Compliance is the deadline. Traceability is the reason. This page covers what serialization actually is, why the API supply chain needed it, and what it costs a bulk drug manufacturer to stay invisible.

The definition

What API serialization is

Serialization is the practice of giving a product a unique identity — a set of standard details that make one pack distinguishable from every other pack, and traceable through the supply chain.

API serialization applies that idea to Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients — bulk drugs, the raw material from which tablets, capsules, creams and injectables are made. Each API pack is assigned a unique identity, encoded in a QR code on its label, so that at any point afterwards you can establish what the pack contains, who made it, when, and where it has been.

A serialization code can be as detailed as a customer or a government requires. In practice it includes the manufacturer's Global Trade Item Number (GTIN), the drug description, the stock-keeping unit, the batch or lot number, the expiry date, the date of production, and the line and plant it was manufactured on.

API, bulk drug, raw material — same thing An Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient is the component of a formulation that produces the intended effect. Everything else in the tablet is excipient. In Indian industry usage it is more often called bulk drug.

Inside the code

What a serialization code carries

Under India's G.S.R. 20(E) guideline the QR code on an API pack must carry eleven specific particulars — from the unique product identification code through to any special storage conditions. Three layers of identity sit underneath them.

Layer 01 · Product

What the material is

The GTIN, the approved product name, the brand name where one exists, and the manufacturer's registered name and address. Held once against the product, applied to every batch of it.

Layer 02 · Batch

Which production run it came from

Batch number and size, date of manufacture, and date of expiry or retest. This is the layer most manufacturers already hold — it is the one that recall notices are written against.

Layer 03 · Container

Which individual drum it is

The Serial Shipping Container Code (SSCC), issued to GS1 international standards and unique to each individual container rather than to the batch. This is the layer that makes traceability granular enough to be useful.

The structural point is the SSCC. A fifty-drum batch produces fifty distinct SSCCs, not one repeated fifty times. That is what lets you isolate a single drum instead of quarantining a whole consignment — and it is the requirement that manual spreadsheet-based numbering fails first.

50 drums = 50 SSCCs One serial per container, never reissued. Batch-level numbering does not satisfy the guideline, and cannot answer the question an auditor actually asks.

The case

Four reasons it matters

The mandate is the deadline. These are the reasons the requirement exists in the first place, and they outlast the compliance date.

1

Counterfeiting is a supply chain problem

Drug counterfeiting is a global issue, and India's scale makes it a target. Where a supply chain has no visibility it has gaps, and gaps are an open invitation to introduce fake or low-quality material. The damage attaches to the manufacturer whose name is printed on the drum.

2

It is how you prove quality and safety

Serialization guarantees the identity of what you shipped. With a standard set of processes behind it, a manufacturer can demonstrate complete supply chain visibility for their material — not assert it, demonstrate it, with a record behind every pack.

3

Problems get found and contained faster

When something does go wrong, a serialized supply chain lets you identify the source quickly and act before the damage widens. Without it you are reconstructing history from paperwork while the affected material keeps moving.

4

Traceability helps everyone downstream

Every member of the chain gains — better inventory management, faster verification of what a drum actually contains, quicker recalls, and fewer supply delays caused by uncertainty about material that cannot be identified with confidence.

What inaction costs

The cost of staying unserialized

The regulatory penalty is the obvious risk. It is rarely the expensive one.

01

Export orders

International buyers increasingly treat scannable traceability as a baseline qualification. A pack that cannot be scanned raises a question at exactly the moment you want none.

02

Audit findings

Inspectors now look for the QR code by default. An observation on an audit report costs far more time to close out than it would have cost to prevent.

03

Customer confidence

Formulation manufacturers are under their own traceability obligations. A supplier who cannot support them creates friction that eventually gets solved by changing supplier.

04

Recall exposure

Without container-level identity, a problem in one drum becomes a problem across the whole consignment, because you cannot prove which drums were unaffected.

At present there is no standard set of rules across the API industry for track and trace, and it remains largely unorganised. That means no visibility in the supply chain, and a large information gap — which is precisely what invites counterfeiters into the market.

Scope

Who this applies to

If you manufacture or import an Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient in India, the QR code requirement applies to you at every packaging level. In practice that means:

API and bulk drug manufacturers

Selling into the domestic market, at every packaging level they ship.

Importers

Bringing API into India, who carry exactly the same obligation as a domestic maker.

Pharmaceutical exporters

Who additionally need GTIN and NDC-standard data for international shipments.

Contract manufacturing organisations

Running multiple product lines for multiple clients, each with its own label specimen.

Quality and regulatory teams

Who have to produce the evidence at the moment an auditor asks for it.
